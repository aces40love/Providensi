STORY 12 / Professional responsibility

Sensitive questions need more than a helpful tone.

Medical and mental-health questions require appropriate evidence and professional care. Simulated chatbot assessments are a reason for boundaries, not a claim that another chatbot is clinically safer.

Providensi insightsOctober 2, 2026Sources linked below
Simulated safety assessmentHistorical testing with material limitationsSafety assessment November 2025; later provider updates

Simulated adolescent conversations are not a clinical outcome trial. Product versions and safeguards change. The assessment also described improvements in explicit crisis handling, which should not be omitted.

What the assessment contributes

Common Sense Media’s November 2025 release describes work with Stanford Medicine’s Brainstorm Lab assessing major chatbots in simulated adolescent mental-health conversations. It reported missed distress cues and weaker behavior in extended exchanges, while also noting improvements on some explicit self-harm prompts.

Later provider announcements, including Meta’s 2026 description of parent alerts, show that safeguards continue to change. An announced feature is not independent proof of effectiveness. Neither type of evidence establishes which product is clinically safe for a particular person.

Keep healthcare operations separate from clinical authority

A practice may have useful administrative work for AI without making it a diagnostic or therapeutic tool. Public-source staff materials, nonclinical procedures, and synthetic workflow prototypes are different from advising an individual patient.

  • Keep diagnoses, treatment, and crisis response with qualified professionals.
  • Do not submit patient information without the required agreements and safeguards.
  • Evaluate specific workflows rather than advertising an assistant as universally safe for health use.
